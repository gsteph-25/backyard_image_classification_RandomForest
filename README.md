<div style='mso-element:para-border-div;border:none;border-bottom:solid #1F3864 1.5pt;

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:11.0pt;border:none;mso-border-bottom-alt:

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:3.5pt;line-height:120%'><b>To:<span
style='mso-spacerun:yes'>  </span></b>Salt Lake City Planning Division</p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:3.5pt;line-height:120%'><b>From:<span
style='mso-spacerun:yes'>  </span></b>Gus Stephens</p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:3.5pt;line-height:120%'><b>Date:<span
style='mso-spacerun:yes'>  </span></b>July 29, 2026</p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:13.0pt;line-height:120%'><b>Re:<span
style='mso-spacerun:yes'>  </span></b>Estimating citywide capacity for detached
accessory dwelling units using aerial imagery, LiDAR, and machine learning</p>

<h1 style='margin-top:17.0pt;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:7.5pt;margin-left:
0in'><b><span style='font-size:13.5pt;color:#1F3864'>Summary</span></b></h1>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:8.0pt;line-height:120%'>Salt Lake City
has allowed accessory dwelling units (ADUs) on single-family lots citywide
since 2018, but the city has not had a parcel-level estimate of how much
backyard space is physically available to build them. This analysis pairs 2024
aerial imagery with 2023 LiDAR elevation data to map open, buildable ground in
the backyard of every non-corner ADU-eligible parcel, after applying the
setback and separation rules in the city's ADU Handbook.</p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:4.5pt;line-height:120%'>Key findings:</p>

<p class=MsoListParagraph style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:
4.5pt;margin-left:23.0pt;text-indent:-13.0pt;line-height:120%;mso-list:l16 level1 lfo25'><![if !supportLists]><span
style='mso-list:Ignore'>•<span style='font:7.0pt "Times New Roman"'>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
</span></span><![endif]><b>23,485 detached ADUs</b> could physically fit in
backyards citywide, spread across 34,535 eligible and ideal parcels.</p>

<p class=MsoListParagraph style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:
4.5pt;margin-left:23.0pt;text-indent:-13.0pt;line-height:120%;mso-list:l16 level1 lfo25'><![if !supportLists]><span
style='mso-list:Ignore'>•<span style='font:7.0pt "Times New Roman"'>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
</span></span><![endif]><b>18,677 of those parcels (80%)</b> have room for both
a minimum-size unit and an off-street parking space in the backyard.</p>

<p class=MsoListParagraph style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:
4.5pt;margin-left:23.0pt;text-indent:-13.0pt;line-height:120%;mso-list:l16 level1 lfo25'><![if !supportLists]><span
style='mso-list:Ignore'>•<span style='font:7.0pt "Times New Roman"'>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
</span></span><![endif]><b>98% of ADU-capable parcels</b> fall within the
transit or bikeway distances that waive the added parking requirement.</p>

<p class=MsoListParagraph style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:
4.5pt;margin-left:23.0pt;text-indent:-13.0pt;line-height:120%;mso-list:l16 level1 lfo25'><![if !supportLists]><span
style='mso-list:Ignore'>•<span style='font:7.0pt "Times New Roman"'>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
</span></span><![endif]>Capacity is unevenly distributed: <b>Council District 7</b>
has the most room (5,338 units) and <b>Council District 4</b> the least (804
units).</p>

<p class=MsoListParagraph style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:
4.5pt;margin-left:23.0pt;text-indent:-13.0pt;line-height:120%;mso-list:l16 level1 lfo25'><![if !supportLists]><span
style='mso-list:Ignore'>•<span style='font:7.0pt "Times New Roman"'>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
</span></span><![endif]><b>Building only the parking-capable subset</b> <b>would
raise the city's housing stock by roughly</b> <b>19%</b>, from 97,318 to
115,995 units (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023).</p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:3.0pt;line-height:120%'>These figures
describe physical capacity, not expected production. They are best used to
quantify the opportunity to build ADUs and to target outreach.</p>

<h1 style='margin-top:17.0pt;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:7.5pt;margin-left:
0in'><b><span style='font-size:13.5pt;color:#1F3864'>I.<span
style='mso-spacerun:yes'>  </span>Background and purpose</span></b></h1>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:8.0pt;line-height:120%'>Salt Lake City
and Utah more broadly face a housing crisis where the supply of housing cannot
keep up with demand (Jeremias, 2024). One way the city responded to this crisis
in 2018 was to update its zoning ordinance to allow accessory dwelling units in
the backyards of single-family zoned properties (Riddle, 2018). An ADU is a
secondary housing unit built on the same lot as a primary residence: a
separate, self-contained living space with its own entrance, kitchen, and bathroom.
ADUs can be detached, attached, or interior (Salt Lake City Planning Division,
n.d.). This analysis addresses detached units only.</p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:8.0pt;line-height:120%'>Knowing where
ADUs are permitted is not the same as knowing whether one will physically fit.
A parcel may be correctly zoned and still have no open ground left once
setbacks, the primary structure, trees, driveways, and paved surfaces are
accounted for. The purpose of this analysis is to close that gap with a
measured, parcel-level estimate.</p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:8.0pt;line-height:120%'>ADUs also offer
several benefits at once. They add housing supply at lower cost than
multifamily construction since there is no land acquisition cost and they tend
to draw less neighborhood opposition than larger projects. ADUs can allow
longtime residents to age in place by moving into the smaller unit while
renting out or sharing the main house. Additionally, they can give homeowners a
path to rental income or equity, and if state law were to permit lot splits and
the sale of ADUs as condominiums, as California now does, they could eventually
serve as entry-level ownership housing (Levin, 2025).</p>

<h1 style='margin-top:17.0pt;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:7.5pt;margin-left:
0in'><b><span style='font-size:13.5pt;color:#1F3864'>III.<span
style='mso-spacerun:yes'>  </span>How land cover was classified</span></b></h1>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:8.0pt;line-height:120%'>The imagery
layers were aligned to a common 50 cm grid, combined into a single image
containing RGB, near-infrared, and height and clipped to the backyard study
areas. A random forest classifier, which is a commonly used machine-learning
method for land cover mapping and has previously been used on NAIP data of Salt
Lake City (Reynolds, 2017), was trained on manually collected sample areas and
then applied to sort every 50 cm cell into one of six categories. In practice,
the model builds hundreds of simple decision rules from areas of known land
cover, then lets each rule “vote” on what every other cell in the image most
likely represents. Combining many independent votes, rather than relying on a
single rule, is what makes the approach reliable across a citywide image with
wide variation in lot size, vegetation, and shadow. Below are the six
classification categories.<span style='mso-spacerun:yes'>  </span></p>

<h1 style='margin-top:17.0pt;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:7.5pt;margin-left:
0in'><b><span style='font-size:13.5pt;color:#1F3864'>IV.<span
style='mso-spacerun:yes'>  </span>Reliability of the classification</span></b></h1>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:8.0pt;line-height:120%'>Accuracy was
measured against independently collected ground-truth areas that the model
never saw during training. Overall accuracy was 86%, yet that figure
understates performance where it counts most: the two categories that drive the
capacity estimate, ILV and NLV/Soil, were correctly identified on the ground
94% and 95% of the time.</p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:8.0pt;line-height:120%'>Height was by
far the most influential input, ranking far above every other variable in the
model's feature importance scores and demonstrating the value of utilizing
LiDAR in an urban planning analysis. Some of the remaining errors sit in
categories that don't affect the total ADU estimate. For instance, some
rooftops are read as tree canopy where overhanging branches and shadow obscure
them, since both are non-buildable classes. Others do affect it directly: some
pavement is read as bare soil, since dry, rocky ground and weathered concrete are
spectrally similar, which is part of why the buildable-area figures should be
read as an upper bound, as discussed below.</p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:10.0pt;line-height:120%'><i><span
style='font-size:10.0pt;line-height:120%;color:#444444'>Producer accuracy is
the share of true ground area of that class that the model correctly found. User
accuracy is the share of the area assigned to that class that was <span
class=GramE>actually correct</span>.</span></i></p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:8.0pt;line-height:120%'>For the two
classes that drive the ADU estimate, ILV and NLV/Soil, producer accuracy
averages roughly 95% while user accuracy averages roughly 85%. These measure
different things, and the gap between them matters for how the citywide total
should be read. Producer accuracy asks: of all the real lawn and bare soil on
the ground, how much did the model find? At 95%, the model is not missing
meaningful amounts of buildable space. User accuracy asks the reverse: of
everything the model labeled buildable, how much of that label can be trusted?
At roughly 85%, about one area in seven labeled “buildable” is <span
class=GramE>actually something</span> else, typically a patch of pavement.
Because the ADU count is built by summing area labeled buildable, this
asymmetry means the citywide estimate is more likely to run slightly high than
slightly low. The 23,485 figure is best read as an upper bound on physical
capacity for all non-corner parcels in the city.</p>

<h1 style='margin-top:17.0pt;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:7.5pt;margin-left:
0in'><b><span style='font-size:13.5pt;color:#1F3864'>VII.<span
style='mso-spacerun:yes'>  </span>Limitations and next steps</span></b></h1>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:4.5pt;line-height:120%'>Several caveats
should travel with these numbers:</p>

<p class=MsoListParagraph style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:
4.5pt;margin-left:23.0pt;text-indent:-13.0pt;line-height:120%;mso-list:l16 level1 lfo25'><![if !supportLists]><span
style='mso-list:Ignore'>•<span style='font:7.0pt "Times New Roman"'>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
</span></span><![endif]><b>This is physical capacity, not forecasted
production. </b>The estimate does not account for owner interest, financing,
utility connections, or landscaping a homeowner wants to keep.</p>

<p class=MsoListParagraph style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:
4.5pt;margin-left:23.0pt;text-indent:-13.0pt;line-height:120%;mso-list:l16 level1 lfo25'><![if !supportLists]><span
style='mso-list:Ignore'>•<span style='font:7.0pt "Times New Roman"'>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
</span></span><![endif]><b>The estimate likely runs slightly high, not low. </b>As
discussed in Section IV, user accuracy for the buildable classes is lower than
producer accuracy, which biases the count toward a modest overstatement of open
ground.</p>

<p class=MsoListParagraph style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:
4.5pt;margin-left:23.0pt;text-indent:-13.0pt;line-height:120%;mso-list:l16 level1 lfo25'><![if !supportLists]><span
style='mso-list:Ignore'>•<span style='font:7.0pt "Times New Roman"'>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
</span></span><![endif]><b>Slope is not yet accounted for. </b>Adding a slope
filter from the digital elevation model that would exclude parcels above
roughly 30 degrees would trim the count of potential ADUs, particularly near
the foothills and in the East Bench.</p>

<p class=MsoListParagraph style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:
4.5pt;margin-left:23.0pt;text-indent:-13.0pt;line-height:120%;mso-list:l16 level1 lfo25'><![if !supportLists]><span
style='mso-list:Ignore'>•<span style='font:7.0pt "Times New Roman"'>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
</span></span><![endif]><b>Corner lots are excluded entirely, </b>so the
citywide figure is an undercount on this front, a separate effect from the
accuracy-driven overestimate discussed above. A corner-lot delineation method
has since been developed and could be incorporated in a future update to
increase the citywide and council district totals. </p>

<p class=MsoListParagraph style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:
4.5pt;margin-left:23.0pt;text-indent:-13.0pt;line-height:120%;mso-list:l16 level1 lfo25'><![if !supportLists]><span
style='mso-list:Ignore'>•<span style='font:7.0pt "Times New Roman"'>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;
</span></span><![endif]><b>Results are least reliable at yard edges </b>where
the imagery resampling method introduces some noise.</p>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:8.0pt;line-height:120%'>For any parcel
where the city intends to act on these results e.g. outreach, a pilot program,
a pre-approved plan set, a site visit or a look at current imagery should
confirm the findings.</p>

<h1 style='margin-top:17.0pt;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:7.5pt;margin-left:
0in'><b><span style='font-size:13.5pt;color:#1F3864'>VIII.<span
style='mso-spacerun:yes'>  </span>Conclusion</span></b></h1>

<p class=MsoNormal style='margin-bottom:8.0pt;line-height:120%'>Combining
aerial imagery with LiDAR-derived height data produces a reliable, citywide
estimate of where detached ADUs will physically fit, at a level of detail that
would be impractical to assemble in person and more accurate than an approach
relying on parcel and building footprint data alone. The result is substantial:
roughly 23,485 units that are already allowed under current zoning, four fifths
of which could accommodate parking as well. Even modest take-up would move the
city meaningfully toward its housing targets, and the parcel-level outputs (shapefile,
excel file, and parcel-level ADU suitability web map) can be handed directly to
outreach, pre-approved plan, or program-design efforts.</p>
